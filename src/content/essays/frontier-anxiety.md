Building at the frontier means building in fog. You can see clearly for a few feet in any direction, but the landscape beyond is speculation. This creates a particular kind of anxiety that I've come to think of as frontier anxiety.

It's different from normal technical uncertainty. When building a web app, the constraints are known. When building with language models that improve monthly, the constraints are moving targets. What seemed impossible last quarter might be trivial today. What you're building carefully today might be obsolete tomorrow.

This is both terrifying and exhilarating.

## The Planning Paradox

Traditional project management assumes stable requirements and predictable timelines. But frontier development inverts this: the requirements evolve based on what turns out to be possible, and timelines depend on breakthroughs that can't be scheduled.

I learned this building PrepVerified. I started with a conventional tutoring system design. Then GPT-4 made sophisticated problem generation feasible. Then function calling enabled structured grading. Each capability shift required redesigning not just the implementation, but the fundamental product concept.

The planning paradox: you need to plan carefully enough to make progress, but loosely enough to pivot when the ground shifts.

## Principles for Foggy Building

**Build for flexibility, not features.** Design systems that can absorb new capabilities rather than systems optimized for current capabilities. This often means over-engineering in directions that seem unnecessary today but enable rapid adaptation tomorrow.

**Prototype relentlessly.** The cost of being wrong about what's possible is high when you're betting months of development time. Build small tests constantly. Most will fail, but the ones that succeed reveal the actual shape of the possible.

**Maintain theoretical debt.** In normal development, you pay down technical debt. In frontier development, you sometimes need to accumulate what I call theoretical debt—building systems based on incomplete understanding of the underlying capabilities, with the expectation of refinancing that debt as theory catches up to practice.

**Optimize for learning speed.** The bottleneck isn't development velocity; it's learning velocity. How quickly can you discover what actually works? How fast can you update your mental model of what's feasible?

## The Emotional Dimension

Frontier anxiety manifests emotionally as a constant low-level doubt: Am I building something useful or just playing with new toys? Am I solving real problems or creating elaborate solutions to problems that won't exist next year?

This doubt is useful when it drives better validation and user research. It becomes paralyzing when it prevents any building at all.

The balance I've found: build things you'd want to exist regardless of the technology. PrepVerified exists because I wanted better learning systems, not because I wanted to play with language models. The AI enables better solutions, but the core problem is technology-independent.

## Temporal Strategy

Frontier builders need to think across multiple time horizons simultaneously:

**Immediate (3 months):** What can I build with current capabilities that creates immediate value?

**Medium-term (1 year):** What capabilities are likely to emerge that would unlock significant new value?

**Long-term (3-5 years):** What becomes possible if current trends continue, and how should that influence architectural decisions today?

Most projects fail by optimizing for only one time horizon. Building only for current capabilities means missing opportunities. Building only for speculated future capabilities means never shipping.

## Embracing the Fog

The fog isn't a bug; it's a feature. Frontier development is compelling precisely because the destination isn't predetermined. You're not just building products; you're discovering what's possible.

This requires a different relationship to uncertainty than most engineering work. Instead of trying to eliminate uncertainty, you learn to navigate it skillfully. You build systems that work with current assumptions while remaining adaptable to future discoveries.

The anxiety never fully goes away, but it transforms. Instead of "I don't know if this will work," it becomes "I don't know exactly how this will evolve, but I'm positioned to evolve with it."

That's the mindset frontier building requires: comfortable with uncertainty, optimistic about adaptation, and always ready to rebuild when the fog lifts and reveals new territory.
