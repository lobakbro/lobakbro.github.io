So "back in my day" as an undergrad doing research in the middle of Boston where it seemed like no matter what direction you were pointing the wind was blowing from that direction, the brain probes we used were sharp hard metal electrodes. The brain is incredibly important for animals. Throughout our lives we bump our heads, take sharp whiplash maneuvers at the red light and we walk away from them without a second thought. We can do that because the brain sits in some salty liquid in your skull, it is a natural dampener. The brain floats in water. This is a problem if you're trying to record from neurons, because the brain shifts in that fluid, and over time the rigid probe tears at the surrounding tissue. The immune response kicks in, scar tissue forms around the electrodes, and eventually the signals degrade until the probe is useless.

For three years in Ed Boyden's lab, I worked on an alternative. The idea was a polymer substrate with a glass transition modulus tuned to body heat and some water absorption, rigid during insertion so you can place it precisely and pierce the brain, but softening once implanted so it moves with the brain instead of against it. The gold electrodes sit on this polymer base. In theory, the probe lasts longer because it stops fighting the tissue.

In practice, the polymer has to survive the brain. The brain is hot, acidic and full of signal unfriendly stuff. Not friendly for delicate devices.

I'm thinking about this now almost a decade later because every big problem is just a bunch of small problems. You want an elegant solution that can do everything. What you hope that you build is something that works good enough so you can keep solving the small problems to make it great.
