They say ideas are cheap. They say that implementation is the hardest part. But now code is cheap. Implementation is cheap. Then why do so many products still suck?

More than ever, you need to know what you want before you build it — before you understand the problem, before you even know if you can build it.

Sure we have docs galore but code diverges from the docs all the time. Plus now that implementation is cheap, you can build stuff you don't know how to do. That's the crux of the problem. You're trying to build something in an area you don't know with design patterns and conventions you don't know. There's no cheat for that. You have to mise-en-place better than you have before. You got to understand the problem, you have to understand the solution, and you have to specify exactly what you want and how you want it to the agent.

I knew I wanted to prep for interviews. But I didn't know what to study. So I started learning about knowledge space theory — the framework behind ALEKS, where experts reason about atomic skills and the prerequisite relationships between them. That's when I realized the real problem wasn't "how do I prompt Claude to generate good questions." It was: what are the best ways to learn? How do you understand someone's knowledge state? How do you present the right type of content in the right way at the right time?

Working with agents forced this clarity. You can't hand-wave at an AI system. If I told the agent "help me prep for ML interviews," I'd get generic flashcard-quality output. To get something useful, I had to articulate: what are the atomic skills? How do they depend on each other? What does it mean to know something versus to have seen it once? What should change about the content when this is a first introduction versus a review?

Knowledge state tracking. Learning style adaptation. The difference between knowledge transfer and knowledge reinforcement. Spaced repetition — not as a feature but as a constraint on when and how content should be served. Interleaving topics to force the brain to discriminate between related concepts. Multiple modalities of looking at the same idea, because learning to code isn't just about code — it's about experiencing a concept in different ways.

The agents don't replace thinking. They demand more of it. Every vague intuition had to become a concrete specification. Every "I'll know it when I see it" had to become a rubric.

This is the thing I think most people get wrong about AI collaboration. The bottleneck isn't the technology's capability — it's the human's clarity about what they're trying to achieve. The agent is a chisel: your problem is a block of marble. You keep picking away at it first with broad strokes and then finely. And the process of getting from fuzzy to precise is itself a form of learning.

The irony is that building a learning system taught me more about learning than any learning system could have.
