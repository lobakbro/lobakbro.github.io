They say ideas are cheap. They say that implementation is the hardest part. But now code (implementation) is cheap. Then why do so many products still suck?

I think the timeline has been compressed so much from ideation to feature release that engineers are jumping to implementation even earlier than before. The process is we go through the thinking steps of design, vet those thoroughly, we build and then repeat. The reality is much more messy and the separation between a good and great engineer, I think, has been communication.

Now that code is cheap, you can build stuff you don't know how to do much more easily than before. That's the crux of the problem. You're trying to build something in an area you don't know much about with design patterns and conventions you don't know. There's no cheat for that. You have to mise-en-place better than you have before. You got to understand the problem, you have to understand the solution, and you have to specify to agents with extreme levels of precision what you want and how you want it.

I knew I wanted to prep for interviews. But I didn't know what to study. So I started learning about knowledge space theory, the framework behind ALEKS, where experts reason about atomic skills and the prerequisite relationships between them. That's when I realized the real problem wasn't "how do I prompt Claude to generate good questions." It was: what are the best ways to learn? How do you understand someone's knowledge state? How do you present the right type of content in the right way at the right time? I don't have all the answers yet, I don't know how everything should work or be implemented; agents won't be able to build what I haven't defined.

You can't hand-wave at an AI system. If I told the agent "help me prep for ML interviews," I'd get generic flashcard-quality output. To get something useful, I had to articulate: what are the atomic skills? How do they depend on each other? What does it mean to know something versus to have seen it once? What should change about the content when this is a first introduction versus a review? Knowledge state tracking. Learning style adaptation. The difference between knowledge transfer and knowledge reinforcement. Spaced repetition not as a feature but as a constraint on when and how content should be served. Interleaving topics to force the brain to suffer maximally so things stick better. Multiple modalities of looking at the same idea, because learning to code better means to look at code not just as code.

Agents don't replace thinking. They demand more of it. Every vague intuition has to become a concrete specification. The solution isn't just to prompt better, it's that that design specifications have grown more important than ever and we need to think collaboratively with agents and think hard about what we intend to build together.
